How to Calculate Freelance Hourly Rate from Salary

Most people start freelancing with the same mental shortcut: they take the full-time salary they want—say, $80,000—and divide it by 2,080 working hours (40 hours times 52 weeks). That gives them $38.46 an hour, so they round it to $40/hr and start pitching clients.

That math breaks down fast. A salaried job quietly pays for things you now cover out of pocket: employer payroll taxes, paid vacation, sick days, software licenses, equipment, and the hours you spend in internal meetings or waiting on the next task. When you work independently, you can't bill 40 hours every single week for 52 weeks straight without burning out—or running out of clients because you never left time for sales and admin.

Here's the grounded formula used by the calculator above:

Hourly Rate = [ (Target Take-Home ÷ (1 − Tax Rate)) + Annual Expenses ] × (1 + Profit Margin) ÷ Annual Billable Hours

A realistic $80,000 salary example

Suppose you want $80,000 in actual take-home pay, expect $5,000 in yearly business expenses, set aside 25% for taxes, plan to take 4 weeks off, bill 25 hours a week, and add a 10% profit buffer. Watch how different the real number is from $38.46/hr:

  1. 1. Gross up take-home pay for 25% taxes ($80,000 ÷ 0.75) $106,667
  2. 2. Add deductible annual business expenses (+ $5,000) $111,667
  3. 3. Add a 10% profit buffer for slow months (× 1.10) $122,833
  4. 4. Calculate billable hours (48 working weeks × 25 hrs) 1,200 hrs
  5. 5. Divide annual revenue by billable hours ($122,833 ÷ 1,200) $102.36 / hr

You need to charge roughly $102/hr (or an $819 day rate) just to live like someone earning an $80,000 W-2 or PAYE salary. That's why experienced consultants rarely price themselves near an employee's base hourly wage.

Practical Pricing Tips

Knowing your minimum rate is step one. Protecting it during client negotiations is what actually keeps your bank account healthy.

  • Budget for 60% billable utilization

    Even if you sit at your desk for 40 hours a week, expect 12 to 15 of those hours to disappear into email, scoping calls, invoicing, and chasing leads. Price around 25 billable hours—if you happen to bill 30, treat the extra income as bonus cash, not your baseline.

  • Move tax money the day an invoice clears

    Open a separate high-yield savings account strictly for taxes. Every time a client pays you, transfer your tax percentage immediately so quarterly filings in the US, UK, Canada, or Australia never catch you short on cash.

  • Stop scope creep before kickoff

    "Quick favors" quietly cut your effective hourly rate in half. Put deliverables, revision limits, and response times in writing, and price extra requests with a simple change order before you start the work.

  • Use your hourly rate internally, quote by project

    Clients don't want to watch the clock—they want a clear price for a clear outcome. Estimate the hours, multiply by your calculator rate, add 15%–20% contingency for surprises, and send a flat project fee or retainer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why shouldn't I just divide my target salary by 2,080 hours?

Dividing a salary by 2,080 assumes you bill 40 hours every week for 52 weeks with zero time off, zero unpaid admin work, and zero overhead. In reality, freelancers cover their own taxes, software, health insurance, vacation, and non-billable sales time. Dividing by 2,080 usually underprices your services by 40% to 60%.

How many billable hours per week is realistic for a freelancer?

Most full-time solo consultants average 20 to 30 billable hours per week over the course of a year. The remaining 10 to 20 hours go toward prospecting, writing proposals, client calls, bookkeeping, and keeping skills current. Starting at 25 billable hours per week is a realistic benchmark.

What tax rate should I enter if I'm self-employed?

A common rule of thumb is 25% to 35% of net profit, depending on your country and income bracket. US contractors pay both federal/state income tax and the 15.3% self-employment tax, while UK, Canadian, and Australian freelancers factor in income tax plus National Insurance, CPP, or superannuation. Check with a local accountant for your exact bracket.

Should I charge an hourly rate, a day rate, or a flat project fee?

Use your calculated hourly rate as an internal floor, not necessarily what you put on every proposal. Hourly billing works well for advisory calls or open-ended support. For defined deliverables, quoting a day rate or fixed project fee based on your target hourly rate rewards you for working efficiently and gives clients budget certainty.

How do I handle unpaid vacation, holidays, and sick days in my rate?

Subtract all non-working weeks from 52 before dividing your annual revenue target. Most independent contractors take at least 4 to 6 weeks off per year once you add up public holidays, sick days, and actual rest. Building that time directly into your rate means you get paid even when you close your laptop.
